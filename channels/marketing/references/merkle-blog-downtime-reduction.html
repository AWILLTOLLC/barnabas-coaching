40% isn't a magic number

When we say embedded IT reduces downtime by 40%, people ask: how do you measure that? What does downtime even mean?

Here's the methodology. Here's what we're measuring. Here's what actually changed.

How we measure downtime

The baseline

Before embedded IT, we measure:

  • Number of unplanned outages per month
  • Average duration of each outage
  • Which systems were affected
  • Which customers/deadlines were impacted
  • How long it took to resolve each issue

This gives us a baseline: "We had 8 outages last month, averaging 2.5 hours each. The LIMS went down 3 times, the network 2 times, the email system 1 time, and the CRM 2 times."

The embedded IT approach

After embedded IT takes over, we measure the same things. But we also add:

  • Which problems were caught before they became outages
  • Which upgrades were done during low-impact windows
  • Which vendors were coordinated proactively
  • Which documentation gaps were filled

This gives us a new baseline: "We had 5 outages this month, averaging 1.5 hours each. The LIMS went down 1 time (down from 3), the network 1 time (down from 2), the email system 1 time (same), and the CRM 1 time (down from 2). We caught 4 problems before they became outages."

The 40% number

8 outages × 2.5 hours = 20 hours of downtime last month
5 outages × 1.5 hours = 7.5 hours of downtime this month

That's a 62% reduction in downtime hours. But we're conservative: 40% is a reasonable estimate that accounts for variability.

What actually changed

1. Proactive monitoring

Before: Systems go down. You call the vendor. They show up in two hours.

After: Systems show warning signs. The embedded IT owner catches them before they become outages.

Example: A server was showing memory errors. The embedded IT owner noticed the pattern. They coordinated a replacement before the server went down. The invoice: "$500 for the upgrade." The cost of the outage: "$5,000 in lost productivity."

2. Vendor coordination

Before: Each vendor sees their own system. The LIMS vendor sees the LIMS. The network vendor sees the network. When they conflict, you're stuck in the middle.

After: The embedded IT owner coordinates between all vendors. They know which system is the bottleneck. They know which upgrade will affect which other system.

Example: The LIMS vendor wanted to upgrade at 2pm on a Friday. The embedded IT owner said "no, do it at 6am on Saturday when the lab is quiet." The upgrade happened without disruption. The invoice: "$0 extra for after-hours work."

3. Documentation

Before: Problems happen. They get fixed. The ticket closes. Six months later, the same problem happens again.

After: Problems happen. They get fixed. The documentation gets updated. The next person knows what happened and how to fix it faster.

Example: The email system went down. The embedded IT owner fixed it. They wrote down what happened, why it happened, and how to fix it next time. The next time it happened, the fix took 15 minutes instead of 2 hours.

4. Context

Before: Your IT vendor knows your systems. They don't know your business.

After: Your embedded IT owner knows your systems and your business. When a system goes down, they know which customers are affected and which deadlines are at risk.

Example: The CRM went down during a client presentation. The embedded IT owner knew which client it was. They coordinated a quick fix. They notified the client. They kept the relationship intact.

Why 40% is conservative

1. The "it depends" factor

Some firms have simpler IT environments. Some have more complex ones. Some have better vendors. Some have worse ones. The 40% number accounts for this variability.

2. The learning curve

The first month of embedded IT is about learning. The second month is about fixing known problems. The third month is about preventing problems. The improvement compounds over time.

3. The "not just IT" factor

Embedded IT isn't just about IT. It's about business. When your IT owner knows your workflows, they can plan around your busy periods. They can schedule upgrades when they'll have the least impact. They can coordinate with your team instead of working around them.

When to consider embedded IT

Strong fit if:

  • Your IT is mission-critical to your business
  • You have 20-200 employees
  • Your systems are complex and evolving
  • Downtime is costing you money (customers, deadlines, productivity)
  • You want someone who takes ownership, not just maintenance

Not the right model if:

  • Your IT needs are simple and stable
  • You're looking for the lowest monthly cost
  • You want a vendor relationship where you never have to think about IT
  • You need 24/7 onsite staff (embedded IT is part-onsite, part-available)
