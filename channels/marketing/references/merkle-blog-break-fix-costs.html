Your IT Is Cheap Until It's Not

Break/fix IT sounds cheap. You pay when things break. When they're working, you pay nothing.

But your IT is cheap until it's not. Then it's expensive in ways you didn't expect.

The first hidden cost: downtime

Your network goes down. Your break/fix vendor shows up in two hours. You've lost a day of productivity. Your customers are waiting. Your team is frustrated.

The invoice says "$250 for two hours of troubleshooting." But you've lost $5,000 in productivity. You've delayed a customer delivery. You've missed a deadline.

The second hidden cost: context

Your break/fix vendor fixes the problem. They close the ticket. They move on.

Six months later, the same problem happens. They fix it again. They close the ticket. They move on.

Who's keeping track of what happened? Who's building a history of your systems? Who's learning from each problem so it doesn't happen again?

The third hidden cost: scope

Your CRM isn't talking to your email system. You call your break/fix vendor. They say it's not in scope. You pay extra to get someone to look at it.

Your network is slow. You call your break/fix vendor. They say it's not in scope. You pay extra to get someone to look at it.

Every time something falls between your systems, you pay for it.

The real cost of "cheap" IT

Downtime by industry

Healthcare: A lab going offline for 4 hours can mean spoiled samples, delayed patient results, and audit findings. Cost: $10,000–$50,000 per incident.

Finance: A trading platform going offline for 30 minutes can mean missed opportunities and client frustration. Cost: $20,000–$100,000+ per incident.

Legal: A document management system going offline before a filing deadline can mean a delayed filing and a missed opportunity. Cost: $5,000–$25,000 per incident.

Manufacturing: A production monitoring system going offline for 2 hours can mean quality issues and wasted materials. Cost: $15,000–$75,000 per incident.

These aren't IT costs. They're business costs. And break/fix IT treats them as afterthoughts.

A case study: the lab that lost 3 weeks of data

The scenario

A biotech lab was using break/fix IT. Their LIMS (Laboratory Information Management System) went offline during a critical data migration. The vendor showed up the next day. They restored the system. They closed the ticket.

What happened

The lab thought the data was safe. It wasn't. Three weeks of data was corrupted. They had to re-run the experiments. They lost $150,000 in research value. They delayed a client delivery by two months.

The invoice

The break/fix vendor sent an invoice for "$3,500 for data restoration." But the real cost was $150,000 in lost research.

What an embedded IT owner would have done

An embedded IT owner would have:

  • Monitored the migration in real-time
  • Caught the error before it corrupted the data
  • Coordinated with the LIMS vendor to fix the root cause
  • Updated the documentation so the next migration would work

The invoice would have been "$5,000 for the month." But they would have saved $150,000 in lost research.

Why break/fix IT keeps breaking

1. No context

Your break/fix vendor sees your systems as a portfolio of problems to solve. They don't know your business. They don't know which systems matter most. They don't know why a problem is urgent.

2. No ownership

Your break/fix vendor fixes the problem. They close the ticket. They move on. They don't own the outcome. They don't care if the problem happens again.

3. No planning

Your break/fix vendor reacts to problems. They don't plan for growth. They don't plan for upgrades. They don't plan for the next stage of your business.

The embedded IT alternative

1. Context matters

An embedded IT owner sits in your office. They meet with your team. They understand your business. When a system goes down, they know which customers are affected and which deadlines are at risk.

2. Ownership vs. maintenance

Your embedded IT owner doesn't just fix problems. They own the outcome. They plan for growth. They upgrade systems before they break. They build a history of your systems so the next person knows what happened.

3. One accountable owner

When something breaks, you know who to call. When you need to upgrade a system, you know who to talk to. When you're planning for growth, you know who to bring in early.

When break/fix IT works

Break/fix works when:

  • Your IT needs are simple and stable
  • You have strong technical people who can manage vendors
  • You're okay with "good enough"
  • You want predictable monthly costs (or no monthly costs)

Break/fix breaks when:

  • Your IT is mission-critical to your business
  • Your systems are complex and evolving
  • Leadership is spending 10+ hours/month on IT coordination
  • You want someone who takes ownership, not just maintenance

The ROI of embedded IT

Downtime reduction

Companies with embedded IT saw 40–50% fewer unplanned outages in year one.

Faster onboarding

New employees get up and running in days, not weeks.

Vendor coordination

One accountable owner means fewer "that's their problem" moments between your vendors.

Audit readiness

When your IT owner knows your workflows, audit prep goes from "where's the documentation?" to "here's what changed last quarter."
